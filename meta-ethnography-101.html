Methodology Basics

Meta-Ethnography 101

A plain-English introduction to the core concepts of meta-ethnography—and how they map onto your workflow in Meta-Ethnography Canvas.

Topics: 1. What Makes It Different? 2. 1st, 2nd & 3rd Order Constructs 3. Choosing an Index Paper 4. Reciprocal, Refutational & LoA 5. The 7 Phases & eMERGe Map
Concept 1

What Makes Meta-Ethnography Different?

Introduced by George Noblit and R. Dwight Hare in 1988, meta-ethnography is the most widely used method for qualitative evidence synthesis (QES) across healthcare, education, social care, and the social sciences.

Unlike a standard narrative review or a descriptive thematic synthesis—which simply groups similar findings into categories—meta-ethnography is an interpretive method. Instead of just asking "What topics appear across these papers?", it asks:

"How do the authors' concepts in Study A relate to the concepts in Study B and Study C—even when the authors use different words—and what new overarching explanation emerges when we put them together?"

Noblit and Hare call this process translation: comparing the concepts and metaphors from one study directly with those of another, while carefully preserving the context (setting, participants, institutional or cultural context) of each original study.

Concept 2

1st, 2nd, and 3rd Order Constructs

Following Schutz's sociology and Britten et al. (2002), modern meta-ethnography distinguishes between three levels ("orders") of data. Keeping these three levels clear is the single most important habit in a meta-ethnography:

🟦 1st Order Constructs — The Participants' Voice Tab 1: Extract & Context (Key: 1)

What they are: Direct quotations from the study participants (e.g., patients, students, teachers, practitioners, community members) reflecting their everyday lived experiences.

Example: "When the prescription arrives on a Friday evening without clinical notes, I feel like I am guessing in the dark." (Participant 4, p. 112)

🟩 2nd Order Constructs — The Primary Authors' Interpretations Tab 1: Extract & Context (Key: 2)

What they are: The themes, concepts, or analytical explanations created by the authors of the primary study to make sense of the participants' quotes. In meta-ethnography, 2nd order constructs are your primary building blocks for synthesis.

Example: The primary authors interpret the quote above as "Structural isolation from the multidisciplinary care team."

🟣 3rd Order Constructs — Your New Synthesis Tab 2: Synthesis Matrix (Rows)

What they are: The new, higher-level interpretations that you (the review team) develop by comparing and translating 2nd order constructs across multiple studies. A good 3rd order construct goes beyond summarizing a topic—it offers a fresh conceptual insight.

Example: Combining findings across 8 studies into a 3rd order construct: "Navigating moral responsibility at the boundary of formal care systems."

💡 Why does the app ask you to link 1st Order quotes to 2nd Order concepts?
In a rigorous meta-ethnography, you are synthesizing the authors' concepts (2nd Order), while keeping the participants' quotes (1st Order) attached—either by dragging quotes onto a 2nd Order card or using the 🔗 Link to 2nd Order... dropdown in Tab 1—so you can verify that the authors' interpretations are grounded in real data and include vivid illustrative quotes in your final manuscript table (Table 2).
Concept 3

Choosing Your "Index Paper" & Ordering Studies

When you start comparing studies in Phase 4 and Phase 5, you need a starting point—called the Index Paper (Study #1). You take the concepts from the Index Paper, compare them with Study #2, then compare that combined understanding with Study #3, and so on.

How do researchers choose an Index Paper?

  • By Conceptual Richness (Most Common): Choose the study that is the most conceptually rich ("thick"), methodologically rigorous, and closely aligned with your review question. (You can rate each paper's Conceptual Depth and Quality in Tab 1 to help you decide!)
  • Chronological Order: Starting with the earliest published paper to see how concepts evolved over time.

In Meta-Ethnography Canvas: Study #1 is automatically marked as your Index paper. Don't worry about picking the "perfect" Index Paper on Day 1—once you have read all your PDFs and rated their richness in Tab 1, simply click Rich★ (to sort by conceptual richness & quality) or Year▲ (to sort chronologically), drag studies manually, or use the ◀ ▶ column buttons in Tab 2. Every order change is automatically logged in your Audit Trail.

Concept 4

Three Ways Studies Relate: Reciprocal, Refutational & Line of Argument

According to Noblit & Hare (1988) and the eMERGe guideline (Items 11–17), studies can relate to each other in three ways. Most real-world syntheses involve a mix of all three:

🤝 1. Reciprocal Translation (When Concepts Agree or Align)

When two or more studies describe essentially the same phenomenon—even if they use different terminology—you "translate" them into one another. For instance, Study A calls it "informal workarounds" and Study B calls it "bending rules for patient safety."

⚡ 2. Refutational Translation (When Concepts Contradict or Diverge)

Often overlooked by beginners, refutational translation examines contradictions, exceptions, or opposing findings across studies. Why did clinicians in Study A feel empowered by a protocol, while clinicians in Study B felt restricted by a similar protocol?

Tip: Keep Show Study Context Row checked in Tab 2 to compare settings and samples across columns, click the 🤝 Reciprocal badge on any deviant card to toggle it to ⚡ Refutational, and use the ⚡ Contradictions Only filter to focus on refutational cases.

🔗 3. Line of Argument Synthesis (Putting the Puzzle Together)

Once you have developed your 3rd Order Themes, you step back and ask: "How do these different themes fit together as parts of a larger whole?" A Line of Argument connects your 3rd order themes into an overarching model, storyline, or theoretical explanation of the phenomenon.

Tip: Click 🔗 Research Aim & Overall Model in Tab 2 to map how your 3rd Order themes connect into a text diagram and overarching narrative (or set an individual row's Translation Relationship to Line of Argument).

Concept 5

How the 7 Phases & eMERGe Map to This App

The table below shows how Noblit & Hare's 7 phases and the 19 reporting criteria of the eMERGe guideline (France et al., 2019) are supported across the workspace:

Phase (Noblit & Hare) eMERGe Criteria Where to Do It in Meta-Ethnography Canvas
Phase 1: Selecting Meta-Ethnography & Getting Started Items 1–4 (Rationale, Aim, Focus & Methodology Choice) Set your Project Code & Phase 1 Research Aim / Review Question in the startup setup modal (or via the top-left project badge), and keep it visible anytime inside the purple drawer in Tab 2 (Synthesis Matrix).
Phase 2: Deciding What Is Relevant Items 5–8 (Search Strategy, Screening & Study Selection) Complete database searching and screening in your preferred SR tool first. Then import your final included full-text PDFs in Tab 1 (Extract & Context) and record quality appraisal (CASP, JBI, MMAT, ETQS, Dixon-Woods, or Custom) and conceptual richness.
Phase 3: Reading Included Studies Items 9–10 (Reading/Extraction & Study Characteristics) Highlight 1st Order quotes and 2nd Order concepts (or add manually for non-OCR PDFs) in Tab 1; auto-fill or edit study context (setting, sample, methods); compare co-reviewer extractions via Compare in the header.
Phase 4: Determining How Studies Are Related Items 11–12 (Process & Outcome of Relating Studies) Choose your Index Paper (#1) using one-click sorting (Rich★ or Year▲) or manual drag-and-drop, and group related 2nd Order concepts into 3rd Order rows in Tab 2 (Synthesis Matrix).
Phase 5: Translating Studies into One Another Items 13–15 (Process & Outcome of Translation) Compare cells across columns while preserving study context in Tab 2, toggle individual cards between 🤝 Reciprocal and ⚡ Refutational, and write cross-study synthesis notes.
Phase 6: Synthesizing Translations Items 16–17 (Synthesis Process, Reflexivity & Line of Argument) Refine 3rd Order Themes, build your Line of Argument text diagram, concept relationship map (savable as .svg) and narrative in the purple drawer, assess GRADE-CERQual confidence, and log reflexive memos in Tab 3 (Audit Trail).
Phase 7: Expressing the Synthesis Items 18–19 (Summary of Findings, Limitations & Conclusions) In Tab 4 (Export Tables), review the auto-compiled eMERGe Methods Summary and checklist mapping, then copy publication-ready tables (Table 1, Table 2, Table 2b Concept × Study Grid, Table 3 CERQual SoQF / Evidence Profile, and Appendix A Audit Trail) as Rich Tables or Markdown, download everything together in Word (.docx), or export the full dataset as Raw CSV.
